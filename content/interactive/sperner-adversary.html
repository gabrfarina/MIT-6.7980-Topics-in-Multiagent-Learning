Find the trichromatic triangle against an adversary

Only the boundary is colored, in the standard way: red on the left side (except the bottom-left corner), yellow along the bottom (except the bottom-right corner), and blue everywhere else. Each small square of the grid is cut into two triangles by its diagonal from top left to bottom right. Click a grid point to ask for its color, or drag to query a whole row or column at once. You win when all three corners of some small triangle have been queried and are red, yellow and blue. The adversary answers honestly, but it fixes the hidden coloring as late as it can.

This game accompanies the query lower bound for Sperner’s lemma in the MIT 6.7980 lecture notes on Brouwer and Sperner.

Why no algorithm wins quickly

Suppose the grid has N + 1 = 2b points per side, so that each coordinate is a b-bit number, and let K = ⌊(N − 4)/12⌋. Every algorithm that learns the coloring only by asking for the colors of points, and always finds a trichromatic triangle, needs at least ⌈(K − 1)/2⌉ ≈ 2b/24 queries on some coloring. That is exponential in b. The lecture notes give the full proof; here is an outline.

The two players

The algorithmAsks for colors. It can be any deterministic algorithm at all: divide and conquer, a clever search, anything.
The adversaryAnswers. We design one adversary and show that it beats every algorithm. Its answers always agree with some legal coloring, so it never cheats.

Step 1 · Tunnel colorings

  • Leave a margin of two points around the grid and cut the rest into horizontal and vertical bands 12 points wide. Where horizontal band k meets vertical band k there is a diagonal square k. There are K of them, one per band: that is one side’s worth, not K². Square 0 is next to the door. In proof mode these are the outlined squares.
  • A tunnel visits diagonal squares 0 → a1 → … → ak. It is drawn as a red wall with yellow on its right-hand side (including the outer corner of every left turn), and blue everywhere else. It enters at the door and ends at the dead end, the center of square ak.
  • A hop from square u to square v runs sideways along the middle of horizontal band u, then up or down along the middle of vertical band v into square v.
  • Where a sideways leg crosses an up-or-down leg, the walls are rewired inside that block so that they never touch. This can split off a closed loop (an island). A loop has no ends, so it holds no trichromatic triangle.
  • Checking a few local pictures (a straight wall, a left turn, a right turn) shows that the only trichromatic triangle is at the dead end. We also checked this by computer for every tunnel through at most 9 diagonal squares.

Step 2 · What one query reveals

Take a point in vertical band x and horizontal band y. Each diagonal square is left at most once, so horizontal band y only carries the hop leaving square y; each square is entered at most once, so vertical band x only carries the hop entering square x. So the color of the point depends only on x, y, the square after y on the tunnel, and the square before x.

Step 3 · The adversary’s strategy

It keeps the tunnel built so far, 0 → a1 → … → ak, and the set of touched diagonal squares, those that some answer depended on. For a query in bands (x, y):

  1. If y is the dead end’s square, extend the tunnel to a diagonal square nobody has touched yet.
  2. Mark x, y, the square after y and the square before x as touched. Touched squares that are not on the tunnel will never join it.
  3. Answer the point’s color in the tunnel as it now stands.

Every answer stays true: a new hop only changes the dead end’s horizontal band and the new square’s vertical band, and no earlier query was in either one.

Step 4 · Counting, for every algorithm

A query touches at most 2 new squares: the square before x and the square after y are already on the tunnel, except for the square that step 1 just added. While some square is untouched, the adversary can still either stop the tunnel where it is or extend it into that square, and the two colorings have their trichromatic triangles in different places. So a correct algorithm cannot stop before it has touched all K squares, which takes at least ⌈(K − 1)/2⌉ queries. Divide and conquer is one such algorithm; it uses about 3N queries, which is in line with the bound.

Step 5 · Exponential

⌈(K − 1)/2⌉ ≈ N/24 ≈ 2b/24. Every extra bit per coordinate doubles N and doubles the number of queries needed.

The grid has about N² = 22b points, but the bound is about N, not N². That is the true answer: divide and conquer wins with about 3N queries, so no proof could force N². Both grow like 2b, so the bound is tight up to a constant.
